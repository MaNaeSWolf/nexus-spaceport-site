The Thesis

Everyone is funding rockets.
Almost nobody is funding
the ground they launch from.

Orbital demand is compounding. Launch supply is capped by a fixed number of ageing, government-built pads. And the specific geography that could relieve that constraint — coastal, near-equatorial, sparsely populated, politically workable — is being consumed by development faster than the market can claim it.

Nexus exists to take that position while it is still available. We have taken the first one: 2,000 hectares of Mozambican coastline, secured.

01 · The Constraint

Demand is compounding.
Supply is physically fixed.

$1.8T

Projected global space economy by 2035, from roughly $630B today — a near-tripling inside a decade.

70,000+

New LEO satellites expected in the next five years, driven by Starlink, Kuiper, OneWeb and sovereign constellations.

76+

New launch vehicles in development or early operation across 20 countries. Every one of them needs pads, ranges and infrastructure.

21

Of those vehicles are fully reusable, with high flight-rate requirements — the category purpose-built infrastructure serves best.

Built by governments.
For government missions.
Ageing. Oversubscribed.

The world's launch capacity sits on infrastructure designed for a different era and a different customer. It cannot be extended indefinitely — pad count, turnaround time and overflight restrictions impose a hard arithmetic limit.

A rare combination — and a closing window

The same coastline that makes an ideal launch site is the most desirable land on Earth for settlement, tourism and industry. Six conditions must hold simultaneously. Very few places on the planet still satisfy all six.

  • 02

    Equatorial

    Near-equatorial latitude maximises orbital energy for GEO, MEO and constellation deployment.

  • 03

    Sparse

    Low population density at the site and across the entire overflight corridor.

  • 04

    Stable

    Predictable weather — fewer scrubs, higher achievable cadence.

  • 05

    Large

    Enough land for multiple pads, assembly buildings and propellant infrastructure with full safety buffers.

  • 06

    Workable

    A regulatory and political environment in which commercial launch licensing is genuinely achievable.

Phase 1 · Primary Site

Morrumbene.
2,000 hectares of
Indian Ocean coast.

Morrumbene sits on Mozambique's Inhambane coast at 23°35′48″S, 35°25′15″E — near-equatorial, ocean-facing and sparsely populated, with open Indian Ocean downrange and a coastline that turns away from the flight path rather than into it. Two thousand hectares are secured, the site is recognised within the national port development programme, and it is the site the entire Nexus programme is built around.

Mozambique also happens to sit on some of the largest proven natural gas reserves in Africa — the feedstock for methane, the propellant the next generation of reusable vehicles is being designed around. That is not a coincidence we discovered later. It is part of why the site was chosen.

What sits on the ground

The facilities are designed to work as one system — delivery, assembly, transport, test, launch and recovery — so that an operator can arrive with a vehicle and leave with a mission flown. This is the full Phase 1 build-out.

Multiple Launch Pads

Thirteen pads in the Phase 1 configuration, sized for high-thrust missions and a range of vehicle classes, with capacity to expand.

Vertical Integration

Integration facilities positioned close to the pads for vehicles requiring vertical assembly or specialised payload configurations.

Horizontal Assembly

Warehousing, cleanrooms and horizontal assembly halls for vehicles built and mated on their side before roll-out.

RTLS Landing Pads

Dedicated return-to-launch-site landing areas beside the pads, enabling the rapid turnaround reusable vehicles depend on.

Downrange Recovery

Downrange landing options and barge recovery operations, giving operators flexibility across mission profiles.

Propellant Production

On-site hydrogen, oxygen and methane via solar-powered electrolysis and pyrolysis, with RP-1 supplied externally. No third-party propellant chain.

Control Centre

Command and control infrastructure for launch monitoring, in-flight tracking and full mission coordination.

Test & Validation

On-site testing facilities so vehicles are validated where they fly, rather than shipped between continents to do it.

Secure Zones

Segregated integration and test zones so one customer's final campaign never interrupts another's operations.

Communications

Telemetry, range communications and satellite tracking support through ascent and early orbit.

Port Access

Integration with a major gas and container port — rockets and oversized ground equipment arrive by sea, not by road across a continent.

Tenant Estate

Industrial and commercial space on the perimeter: manufacturing, warehousing, offices and accommodation, let to operators and suppliers.

04 · Discipline

Safety and compliance
are the moat.

In launch infrastructure the barrier to entry is not concrete. It is the licence, the safety case and the export-control architecture. Nexus treats all three as core engineering work, from the first hectare acquired.

Safety is designed in,
not bolted on

Nexus sized its exclusion envelopes, acoustic zones and land requirements before a single hectare was acquired — which is why the site is as large as it is. Safety determined the footprint; the footprint did not determine the safety case.

Land First
Buffer distances drove site selection and acquisition, rather than being retro-fitted to land that happened to be available.
Per Vehicle
Every launch campaign is assessed on its own merits. Vehicle-specific analysis, independently reviewed, precedes any flight from a Nexus pad — no mission proceeds on generic assumptions.
Independently Reviewed
Internal engineering work is written to be handed to third-party reviewers and host-country regulators, not to satisfy ourselves.
Ocean Downrange
Every site was selected for open-water corridors and low population exposure — the cheapest safety margin available is the one geography gives you for free.

Compliance as competitive advantage

The global aerospace industry is fragmenting into overlapping compliance ecosystems. Export control discipline, operational compartmentalisation and legal ringfencing are strategic capabilities at Nexus, not afterthoughts.

ITAR Dual-Zone
Each site operates two fully segregated zones — ITAR-controlled and ITAR-free — with independent physical infrastructure, data networks, telemetry and access control. US and non-US clients can be served simultaneously without cross-contamination.
European Controls
EU Dual-Use Regulation is treated as a separate compliance layer from ITAR. Less extraterritorial in effect, but tightening — particularly on China-facing aerospace cooperation.
US Subsidiary
A separately incorporated US subsidiary operating under a Special Security Agreement preserves eligibility for NASA and US civil agency work while maintaining corporate separation.
MTCR & Africa
The MTCR's explicit civilian carve-out enables launch infrastructure development. Nexus works with non-restricted infrastructure suppliers where possible and maintains transparent, commercial-only positioning.

Accepted launch vehicle policy

Rocket-agnostic, with clearly drawn boundaries. Operators know exactly what the facility will and will not host before they design around it.

Propellants
AcceptedLH₂, LOX and LCH₄ produced on-site via solar-powered electrolysis and pyrolysis. RP-1 supplied externally and available at the facility.
Solid Boosters
AcceptedSolid-fuelled boosters and strap-ons up to a maximum total solid propellant mass of 600 tonnes per vehicle configuration.
Toxic Propellants
Not acceptedHighly toxic propellants — including hydrazine-based monopropellants and hypergolics such as NTO and UDMH — are not supported.
Military Payloads
Not acceptedNexus facilities are strictly commercial and politically neutral. Military payloads, weapons systems and classified government missions are excluded.
Vehicle Origins
All nationsSubject to applicable export controls and the dual-zone compliance architecture. Commercial operators from any nation may use the ITAR-free zones.
05 · Why Nexus

The rocket business is a race.
The ground beneath it
is a position.

Six reasons capital belongs in launch infrastructure rather than — or alongside — launch vehicles.

01

You do not have to pick the winner

Of 76+ vehicle programmes in development, most will fail. Infrastructure is indifferent to which ones. A spaceport earns from every operator that flies from it, and its addressable customer base grows every time a new programme is announced — including the ones that eventually beat each other.

02

The asset is the geography

Land, licence and a clean over-ocean corridor at a near-equatorial coastal site is the genuinely scarce input in this industry. It cannot be manufactured, out-engineered or capital-accelerated. It can only be acquired — and the remaining inventory is finite, identifiable, and shrinking.

03

Fixed asset, multiplied revenue

One pad complex serves many tenants across five independent income lines simultaneously. Utilisation, not construction, drives the return curve — and utilisation is set by a demand wave that is already in motion and largely visible on public manifests.

04

Infrastructure-shaped cash flows

Annual pad leases and multi-year service agreements produce contracted, recurring revenue with the duration profile of an airport or a port — not the lumpy, mission-by-mission economics of a launch provider.

05

Neutrality is a market, not a slogan

A growing set of credible commercial and sovereign operators are locked out of US, Chinese and European ranges by politics or export control. They are not short of capital or vehicles. They are short of somewhere to launch from. Nexus is built specifically to be that place.

06

A platform, not a project

Three sites across two continents, three ocean corridors and both hemispheres — a range of inclinations no single location can reach, available to one customer under one operator, one compliance regime and one contract. That is a network effect a single-site competitor cannot answer.

Why now — the 2026–2030 window

Demand Surge
Global launch cadence is accelerating toward the physical ceiling of existing infrastructure. New capacity has to exist before 2030 to be relevant to it.
Geography Scarcity
Ideal coastal-equatorial sites are being consumed by settlement and industry. The window to secure prime locations is closing on a schedule nobody controls.
Commercial Shift
Governments are stepping back from operating launch ranges. Private operators need neutral, accessible infrastructure not controlled by geopolitical rivals.
Reusable Vehicles
High-cadence reusable vehicles need infrastructure designed for fast turnaround. Cold War ranges cannot support it, and cannot be economically retrofitted to.
Build Cost Curve
Building during the ramp-up is materially cheaper than building at the ceiling, when construction demand peaks and timelines compress.
