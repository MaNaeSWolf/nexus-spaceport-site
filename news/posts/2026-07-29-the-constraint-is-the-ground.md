Orbital demand is compounding. Launch supply is not.

The world's launch capacity sits on a fixed number of ageing, government-built
pads designed for a different era and a different customer. Pad count, turnaround
time and overflight restrictions impose a hard arithmetic limit that cannot be
extended indefinitely.

## The arithmetic

- **United States** — Cape Canaveral and Vandenberg account for more than half of
  global launches. Every Cape pad is currently occupied, with the physical
  ceiling reached around 2030.
- **China** — Wenchang is capped near 120 launches a year, and inland sites are
  constrained by population overflight.
- **Europe** — three orbital launches in 2024, behind Japan, India and Iran.
- **Russia** — removed from the commercial market following the invasion of
  Ukraine.

Against that, more than 76 new launch vehicles are in development or early
operation across 20 countries. Twenty-one of them are fully reusable, with
high flight-rate requirements that Cold War infrastructure cannot economically
be retrofitted to support.

## The position

An airport does not fly the aeroplanes. It is indispensable regardless.

Of those 76 vehicle programmes, most will fail. Infrastructure is indifferent to
which ones — a spaceport earns from every operator that flies from it, and its
addressable customer base grows every time a new programme is announced,
including the ones that eventually beat each other.

Land, licence and a clean over-ocean corridor at a near-equatorial coastal site
is the genuinely scarce input in this industry. It cannot be manufactured,
out-engineered or capital-accelerated. It can only be acquired — and the
remaining inventory is finite, identifiable, and shrinking.
